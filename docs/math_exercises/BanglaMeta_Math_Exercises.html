BanglaMeta: Mathematics Exercises

20 exam-style problems with worked solutions. Each problem uses small numbers you can compute by hand, and its result demonstrates one formula or theorem behind the merged Bangla Word2Vec model.

How to use this set. Cover the green solution box, solve parts (a), (b), (c) yourself, then compare. The yellow box says what the result proves. Problems 9, 11 and 17 use real numbers read from the model files and the thesis results, so you can check them in the web app (Explorer → Relations) and in eval/conf_output.
Every number in every solution was computed by docs/math_exercises/build_exercises.py, which also checks each claim with an assertion before writing this document.

Notation

$\hat x = x/\lVert x\rVert$ (unit length) · $\cos(u,v)=u\cdot v/(\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert)$ · $[x;y]$ = vectors placed one after the other · $m(w)$ = merged row (thesis) · $s(w)$ = similarity-view row · $c(w)$ = BanglaMeta+ row · $R(H)$ = lowest loss reachable by classifiers in $H$ · $H(\cdot)$ = entropy in nats · $I(Y;X_B\mid X_A)$ = conditional mutual information.

Building one merged row

Merge formula (§2): normalise, then concatenate with weight 1/√K

A word w has these raw rows in three source models (dimensions are kept small for hand work):

$$n(w) = (3,\ 4),\qquad i(w) = (1,\ 2,\ 2),\qquad s(w) = (0,\ 5,\ 12).$$

(a) Normalise each row to length 1. (b) Form the merged row $m(w)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}[\hat n;\hat i;\hat s]$. (c) Show that $\lVert m(w)\rVert = 1$ and that each source supplies exactly one third of the squared length.

Solution

(a) $\lVert n\rVert=\sqrt{9+16}=5$, $\lVert i\rVert=\sqrt{1+4+4}=3$, $\lVert s\rVert=\sqrt{0+25+144}=13$, so $\hat n=(0.6,\,0.8)$, $\hat i=(\tfrac13,\tfrac23,\tfrac23)$, $\hat s=(0,\tfrac{5}{13},\tfrac{12}{13})$.

(b) Multiply every entry by $1/\sqrt3 = 0.5774$:

$$m(w) = (0.3464,\ 0.4619,\ 0.1925,\ 0.3849,\ 0.3849,\ 0.0000,\ 0.2221,\ 0.5329).$$

(c) Squared length of each block: $\tfrac13\lVert\hat n\rVert^2=\tfrac13$, likewise $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac13$ (computed: 0.3333, 0.3333, 0.3333). Total $=\tfrac13+\tfrac13+\tfrac13=1$, so $\lVert m(w)\rVert = 1$.

Shows: after Step 1 and Step 3 every merged row has length exactly 1 and no source can dominate by scale: each contributes one third. With $K$ sources the factor $1/\sqrt K$ gives $K\cdot\tfrac1K = 1$.

Filling a missing word with the ridge map

Merge formula, Step 2: $M=(B^\top B+\lambda I)^{-1}B^\top A$, $\lambda=1$

Three words are known by both models. Their rows (one word per row) are

$$B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

(Model A happens to be model B turned by 90°.) A fourth word is known only by B, with normalised row $b=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2,1)$.

(a) Compute $M=(B^\top B+I)^{-1}B^\top A$. (b) Predict and normalise the missing A-row $\tilde a = bM/\lVert bM\rVert$. (c) Compare with the exact 90° answer and with $\lambda = 0$.

Solution

(a) $B^\top B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, so $B^\top B+I=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$, whose inverse is $\tfrac18\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix}$ (determinant $9-1=8$). $B^\top A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Therefore

$$M=\tfrac18\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-1&2\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac18\begin{pmatrix}-1&5\\-5&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

(b) $bM=\tfrac{1}{8\sqrt5}(2\cdot(-1)+1\cdot(-5),\ 2\cdot5+1\cdot1)=\tfrac{1}{8\sqrt5}(-7,\,11)$; after normalising, $\tilde a=(-7,11)/\sqrt{170}=(−0.5369,\ 0.8437)$.

(c) The exact turned row is $(-1,2)/\sqrt5=(−0.4472,\ 0.8944)$. Cosine between them $=29/\sqrt{850}=0.9947$. With $\lambda=0$: $(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, the exact 90° turn.

Shows: the ridge map learns the relation between the two models from shared words, and a missing row is predicted almost exactly (cosine 0.9947). $\lambda$ only shrinks the map; the final normalisation removes the shrinkage of length, so the direction stays close (Theorem 8 bounds the remaining error).

Sentence vector of a merged model

Theorem 1 (pooling identity), check P1

A two-word sentence. In model A the rows are $a_1=(1,0)$, $a_2=(0.6,0.8)$; in model B they are $b_1=(0,1)$, $b_2=(1,0)$. The merged rows are $m_j=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}[a_j;b_j]$.

(a) Compute the sentence vector $x_m=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)$. (b) Compute $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}[x_A;x_B]$ where $x_A,x_B$ are the sentence vectors of each model. (c) Compare.

Solution

(a) $m_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,0,0,1)$, $m_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(0.6,0.8,1,0)$, so $x_m=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(0.8,0.4,0.5,0.5)=(0.5657,\ 0.2828,\ 0.3536,\ 0.3536)$.

(b) $x_A=\tfrac12(a_1+a_2)=(0.8,0.4)$, $x_B=\tfrac12(b_1+b_2)=(0.5,0.5)$, so $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}[x_A;x_B]=(0.5657,\ 0.2828,\ 0.3536,\ 0.3536)$.

(c) Identical, entry by entry (the thesis check P1 measured an error of exactly 0.0 on 500 texts).

Shows: averaging, concatenating and scaling are all linear, so they can be done in any order. The merged sentence vector is the two sources' sentence vectors placed side by side, which is what Theorems 2–5 rely on.

Merging cannot lose

Theorem 2: $R(H_{AB})\le\min(R(H_A),R(H_B))$

Four texts have one feature from each model and a label:

text$x_A$$x_B$label $y$
111−1
213+1
331+1
422+1

A linear classifier predicts $\operatorname{sign}(w\cdot x+c)$. (a) Find the fewest errors any classifier using only $x_A$ can make. (b) The same for $x_B$. (c) Find a classifier on $[x_A;x_B]$ with no error. (d) Write the best $x_A$-only classifier as a classifier on $[x_A;x_B]$.

Solution

(a) Texts 1 and 2 both have $x_A=1$ but different labels, so any rule based on $x_A$ alone gives them the same answer and gets at least one wrong. The cut $x_A>1.5$ makes exactly 1 error (text 2): error rate $1/4$.

(b) Texts 1 and 3 both have $x_B=1$ with different labels: at least 1 error, error rate $1/4$.

(c) $\operatorname{sign}(x_A+x_B-3)$: sums are $2,4,4,4$, giving $-,+,+,+$: 0 errors.

(d) $\operatorname{sign}(1\cdot x_A-1.5)=\operatorname{sign}([1,\ 0]\cdot[x_A;x_B]-1.5)$: the same rule with weight 0 on $x_B$.

Shows: every rule on one model is also a rule on the merged features (part d), so the merged model's best error can never be higher ($0 \le \min(1/4,1/4)$). Here it is strictly lower, because each model fixes the other's mistake.

How big is the gain? Conditional information

Theorem 3: $R^*(A)-R^*(A,B)=I(Y;X_B\mid X_A)\ge 0$

$X_A$ and $X_B$ are independent fair coins (0/1) and the label is $Y = X_A \text{ AND } X_B$. A predictor is scored by log-loss $-\ln p(\text{true label})$, in nats.

(a) Compute $H(Y\mid X_A)$. (b) Compute $H(Y\mid X_A,X_B)$. (c) Give the gain from adding $X_B$. (d) What is the gain if $X_B$ is just a copy of $X_A$?

Solution

(a) If $X_A=0$, then $Y=0$ for sure: entropy 0. If $X_A=1$, then $Y=X_B$, a fair coin: entropy $\ln 2$. So $H(Y\mid X_A)=\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\ln2=0.3466$ nats. The best predictor using $X_A$ only says "$Y=1$ with probability 0.5" when $X_A=1$, and its average log-loss is exactly this 0.3466.

(b) Given both coins, $Y$ is known: $H(Y\mid X_A,X_B)=0$.

(c) Gain $= 0.3466-0 = 0.3466$ nats $= I(Y;X_B\mid X_A)$.

(d) Then $X_B$ tells nothing new: $H(Y\mid X_A,X_B)=H(Y\mid X_A)$ and the gain is 0, never negative.

Shows: the improvement from merging equals the label information the second model adds beyond the first. It is zero only when the second model is redundant. The thesis measured it before merging (check P2): +0.068 nats on informal text for News-W2V + Informal-W2V, and +0.003 (not different from 0) for adding Subs2vec as a third model.

Why not just average the two models?

Theorem 4: concatenation dominates every linear merge $z=Lm$

Two kinds of text. For class $+$ the sentence features are $x_A=1,\ x_B=-1$; for class $-$ they are $x_A=-1,\ x_B=1$.

(a) Average the models: $z=\tfrac12(x_A+x_B)$. Can any classifier on $z$ separate the classes? (b) Find a classifier on the concatenation $[x_A;x_B]$ that does. (c) Show that any classifier on $z=Lx$ is also a classifier on $x$.

Solution

(a) Class $+$: $z=\tfrac12(1-1)=0$. Class $-$: $z=\tfrac12(-1+1)=0$. Both classes map to the same number, so every classifier on $z$ gives them the same answer: at best 50% correct.

(b) $\operatorname{sign}(1\cdot x_A + 0\cdot x_B)$ gives $+1$ and $-1$: 100% correct.

(c) A classifier on $z$ computes $w\cdot z = w\cdot(Lx) = (wL)\cdot x$, which is the classifier with weights $wL$ on $x$. So the set of classifiers on $z$ is contained in the set on $x$, and its best loss cannot be lower.

Shows: averaging ($L=[\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$), SVD ($L=V_k^\top$), and rotation followed by averaging are all of the form $z=Lm$, so none can beat concatenation in reachable loss, and averaging can destroy information completely. This is why the thesis used concatenation (and DEV confirmed it: CONC 76.97 > SVD-300 76.36 > Procrustes+AVG 76.28).

Block weights do not change the classifier

Theorem 5: standardisation cancels any weight $c>0$

One feature column over four texts is $x=(1,2,3,6)$. A block weight multiplies it by $c=0.5$, giving $(0.5,1,1.5,3)$. The probe standardises each column: $z=(x-\bar x)/\sigma_x$ (population standard deviation).

(a) Standardise $x$. (b) Standardise $0.5x$. (c) Explain the result for any $c>0$.

Solution

(a) $\bar x=3$; deviations $-2,-1,0,3$; variance $(4+1+0+9)/4=3.5$; $\sigma_x=1.8708$. So $z=(−1.0690,\ −0.5345,\ 0.0000,\ 1.6036)$.

(b) Mean $1.5$; deviations $-1,-0.5,0,1.5$; variance $0.875$; $\sigma=0.9354$. So $z=(−1.0690,\ −0.5345,\ 0.0000,\ 1.6036)$: the same.

(c) $\overline{cx}=c\bar x$ and $\sigma_{cx}=c\,\sigma_x$, so $\dfrac{cx-c\bar x}{c\,\sigma_x}=\dfrac{x-\bar x}{\sigma_x}$: $c$ cancels.

Shows: the classifier sees identical inputs for every weight $c>0$, so the merged model's accuracy needs no weight tuning. (The thesis ran $\alpha = 0.3, 0.5, 0.7$ and got identical F1 76.97.) The same fact lets BanglaMeta+ give the thesis rows a tiny weight without losing their classification power. A weight of exactly 0 is different: it deletes the feature (Problem 9).

Merged similarity is the average of the sources' similarities

Theorem 6 and its corollary

Two words $u,v$. Model A rows: $a_u=(1,0)$, $a_v=(0.6,0.8)$. Model B rows: $b_u=(0.6,0.8)$, $b_v=(0,1)$. Merged rows $m=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}[a;b]$.

(a) Compute $\cos_A$, $\cos_B$ and $\cos(m_u,m_v)$. (b) Each model's similarity has an error with standard deviation $\sigma=0.10$, correlation $\rho=0.3$. What is the error standard deviation of the merged similarity? (c) Repeat with $\sigma_A=0.05$, $\sigma_B=0.30$, $\rho=0$.

Solution

(a) $\cos_A=1\cdot0.6+0\cdot0.8=0.60$, $\cos_B=0.6\cdot0+0.8\cdot1=0.80$. Merged: $m_u\cdot m_v=\tfrac12(a_u\cdot a_v)+\tfrac12(b_u\cdot b_v)=\tfrac12(0.60+0.80)=0.70$.

(b) Error of the average $=\tfrac12(e_A+e_B)$, variance $\tfrac14(\sigma^2+\sigma^2+2\rho\sigma^2)=\sigma^2\tfrac{1+\rho}2=0.0065$, standard deviation $0.0806 < 0.10$: less noise than either model.

(c) Variance $\tfrac14(0.05^2+0.30^2)=0.023125$, standard deviation $0.1521$, worse than model A alone (0.05).

Shows: equal-weight merging averages similarities. That reduces noise when the models are equally good, but a much noisier model drags the average down (part c). This is exactly why the thesis BanglaMeta scored below News-W2V on word similarity (Informal-W2V is the noisy model) and why BanglaMeta-Sim re-weights the blocks (Problem 9).

Block weights for word similarity (BanglaMeta-Sim)

Weighted cosine $\cos_a=\tfrac13\sum_k a_k\cos_k$, $\tfrac13\sum a_k=1$

The merged row has three unit blocks, each scaled by $1/\sqrt3$. Giving block $k$ weight $a_k$ (with the weights averaging 1) means the row becomes $[\sqrt{a_k/3}\,\hat x_k]_k$.

(a) Show the weighted row still has length 1 when $\tfrac13(a_1+a_2+a_3)=1$, and that the cosine of two words becomes $\tfrac13\sum_k a_k\cos_k$. (b) Block cosines of two words are $\cos_N=0.70$, $\cos_I=0.10$ (noisy Informal block), $\cos_S=0.50$. Compute the cosine with equal weights and with the BanglaMeta-Sim weights $a=(1.65,\ 0.00,\ 1.35)$. (c) Real data: the block cosines of রাজা and পুরুষ in the thesis model are $0.203,\ 0.364,\ 0.145$. Compute their merged cosine.

Solution

(a) Squared length $=\sum_k \tfrac{a_k}{3}\lVert\hat x_k\rVert^2=\tfrac13\sum_k a_k=1$. The dot product of two such rows is $\sum_k\tfrac{a_k}3\,\hat x_k\cdot\hat y_k=\tfrac13\sum_k a_k\cos_k$.

(b) Equal: $\tfrac13(0.70+0.10+0.50)=0.4333$. Weighted: $\tfrac13(1.65\cdot0.70+0.00\cdot0.10+1.35\cdot0.50)=0.6100$.

(c) $\tfrac13(0.203+0.364+0.145)=0.237$: exactly the value the web app shows for রাজা–পুরুষ with "Thesis rows".

Shows: block weights move the similarity toward the reliable blocks. Weight 0 removes the noisy Informal block from word similarity. Weights are free for classification (Problem 7) but not for similarity, so they were chosen on half of the analogy questions and tested on the other half (analogy 5.62% → 8.66% on the unseen half, after the next step).

Removing the common direction (all-but-the-top)

Post-processing used by BanglaMeta-Sim

Word vectors often share one big common direction that says nothing about meaning. Here it is $u=(1,0,0)$:

$$\text{king}=(4,1,0),\quad \text{queen}=(4,1,0.5),\quad \text{rice}=(4,0,1).$$

(a) Compute cos(king, queen) and cos(king, rice). (b) Remove the common direction, $v' = v-(v\cdot u)u$, and compute both cosines again. (c) Which version separates related from unrelated words better?

Solution

(a) cos(king, queen) $=\dfrac{16+1+0}{\sqrt{17}\sqrt{17.25}}=0.9927$; cos(king, rice) $=\dfrac{16}{\sqrt{17}\sqrt{17}}=0.9412$. Gap $=0.0516$.

(b) king′ $=(0,1,0)$, queen′ $=(0,1,0.5)$, rice′ $=(0,0,1)$. cos(king′, queen′) $=1/\sqrt{1.25}=0.8944$; cos(king′, rice′) $=0$. Gap $=0.8944$.

(c) After removal: the related pair stays high and the unrelated pair falls to 0.

Shows: subtracting the mean and the top shared directions (here one; BanglaMeta-Sim removes 4 per block) makes cosine reflect meaning instead of a common offset. On real data this raised the similarity AUC from 0.819 to 0.891 and is why unrelated pairs such as রাজা–ভাত drop near 0 (0.036) in the app.

One model for both jobs (BanglaMeta+)

$c(w)=[\sqrt\varepsilon\,m_{NI}(w);\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\,s(w)]$, $\cos_c=\varepsilon\cos_{NI}+(1-\varepsilon)\cos_s$

BanglaMeta+ joins the thesis News+Informal part $m_{NI}(w)$ and the similarity row $s(w)$ (both length 1) with $\varepsilon = 0.0005$.

(a) Show that $\lVert c(w)\rVert = 1$. (b) Show $\cos_c(u,v) = \varepsilon\cos_{NI}(u,v)+(1-\varepsilon)\cos_s(u,v)$. (c) Real data for রাজা and রানী: $\cos_{NI}=0.5883$, $\cos_s=0.3368$. Compute $\cos_c$. (d) Why does the classifier keep the full accuracy of the thesis rows, although their weight is only $\varepsilon$?

Solution

(a) $\lVert c\rVert^2=\varepsilon\lVert m_{NI}\rVert^2+(1-\varepsilon)\lVert s\rVert^2=\varepsilon+1-\varepsilon=1$.

(b) $c(u)\cdot c(v)=\varepsilon\, m_{NI}(u)\cdot m_{NI}(v)+(1-\varepsilon)\, s(u)\cdot s(v)$, and all parts have length 1, so dot product = cosine.

(c) $0.0005\times0.5883 + 0.9995\times0.3368 = 0.3369$. Read directly from the BanglaMeta+ file: $0.3369$, which the app shows as 0.337.

(d) Multiplying the thesis features by $\sqrt\varepsilon$ is a block weight; standardisation cancels it (Problem 7), so the classifier sees all thesis features at full strength, plus the similarity features.

Shows: one vector per word can carry both strengths. Word similarity is 99.95% the tuned view (analogy 8.98%, AUC 0.891) while classification keeps the thesis News+Informal information (DEV macro-F1 77.34; held-out CONF 77.22, against 77.17 for the thesis model). Subs2vec is left out of the thesis part because it adds no significant classification information.

Analogy: a relation is a direction

3CosAdd: answer $=\arg\max_x \cos(x,\ b-a+c)$, inputs excluded

Dimensions are (royal, male, female). Unit rows: king $=(0.8,0.6,0)$, queen $=(0.8,0,0.6)$, man $=(0,1,0)$, woman $=(0,0,1)$, rice $=(0.6,0,0.8)$.

(a) Solve king : queen :: man : ? with 3CosAdd. (b) Compute cos(king, man). (c) Compare the directions queen − king and woman − man.

Solution

(a) Target $t=$ queen − king + man $=(0.0,\ 0.4,\ 0.6)$, $\lVert t\rVert=0.7211$. Candidates (king, queen, man excluded): cos(woman, $t$) $=0.8321$, cos(rice, $t$) $=0.6656$. Answer: woman.

(b) cos(king, man) $=0.8\cdot0+0.6\cdot1+0\cdot0=0.6$: only moderately similar.

(c) queen − king $=(0,-0.6,0.6)$, woman − man $=(0,-1,1)$: cosine $1.0$, the same direction.

Shows: a relation such as male → female is a direction shared by many pairs, not a high similarity between the words. This is why রাজা–পুরুষ has a low cosine in every model, yet রাজা : রানী :: পুরুষ : ? returns মহিলা in the app.

Sentence vector and coverage

Sentence vector = mean of the known word rows

A sentence has three words. The model knows two of them, with rows $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$; the third is missing from the vocabulary.

(a) Compute the sentence vector and the coverage. (b) Compute its cosine with a second sentence whose vector is $(0.6,0.8)$. (c) What would happen if a missing word were given a made-up vector?

Solution

(a) Mean of the known rows $=(0.5,0.5)$; coverage $=2/3=0.667$.

(b) $\dfrac{0.5\cdot0.6+0.5\cdot0.8}{\sqrt{0.5}\cdot1}=\dfrac{0.7}{0.7071}=0.9899$.

(c) It would move the mean in an arbitrary direction and change every score built on it. Leaving it out keeps the vector equal to what the model actually knows.

Shows: the app reports coverage and marks missing words instead of inventing vectors, the same rule the thesis used for every evaluation.

Same similarity, different list positions

Cosine is symmetric; a neighbour rank is one-way

Four words have these cosines (the matrix is a valid set of unit vectors: all its eigenvalues are positive):

ABCD
A10.50.90.8
B0.510.20.1
C0.90.210.75
D0.80.10.751

(a) What is cos(A, B) and cos(B, A)? (b) Where is B in A's list of nearest words? Where is A in B's list?

Solution

(a) Both are 0.5; the dot product does not depend on the order.

(b) A's list: C (0.9), D (0.8), B (0.5), so B is #3. B's list: A (0.5), C (0.2), D (0.1), so A is #1.

Shows: A has many close neighbours and B has few, so the two list positions differ although the similarity is one number. The app's Relations tab therefore shows the cosine as "same both ways" and labels ranks as one-way.

Similarity AUC by counting

AUC = share of (same-category, different-category) pairs ranked correctly

Three word pairs from the same category and four from different categories get these cosines.

Model 1: same $\{0.80, 0.60, 0.55\}$, different $\{0.70, 0.40, 0.30, 0.20\}$.
Model 2: same $\{0.62, 0.45, 0.40\}$, different $\{0.10, 0.05, 0.02, -0.08\}$.

(a) Compute each model's AUC: the share of the $3\times4=12$ (same, different) comparisons in which the same-category pair scores higher.

Solution

Model 1: 0.80 beats all 4; 0.60 beats 3 (not 0.70); 0.55 beats 3. Total $10/12=0.8333$.

Model 2: every same-category score is above every different-category score: $12/12=1.0000$.

Shows: AUC measures ranking, not the size of the numbers. Model 2 has lower cosines but separates perfectly, like BanglaMeta-Sim after removing the common direction (Problem 10): its cosines are smaller, its AUC higher (0.891 vs 0.819).

How much can selection on DEV be trusted?

Theorem 10: margin $=\sqrt{\ln(2K/\delta)/(2n)}$

$K=28$ candidate models are compared on $n=5{,}950$ texts, with failure probability $\delta=0.05$.

(a) Compute the margin within which all 28 measured accuracies are guaranteed. (b) Repeat for $K=3$. (c) On DEV the selected model scored 77.12 and the runner-up (Q: CAEME) 77.08, a difference of 0.04 points. Is this order guaranteed?

Solution

(a) $2K/\delta=1120$, $\ln1120=7.0211$, $2n=11{,}900$: margin $=\sqrt{7.0211/11900}=0.0243$, about 2.4 points.

(b) $\ln(120)=4.7875$: margin $=0.0201$ (2.0 points). More candidates, wider margin, but only slowly ($\sqrt{\ln K}$).

(c) No: 0.04 points is far below the 2.4-point margin, so this bound cannot guarantee the order.

Shows: picking the best of 28 on the same data cannot by itself prove the winner is better. That is why the thesis froze the selected model and tested it once on the untouched CONF half with only 3 pre-registered comparisons.

Holm correction of the three confirmatory tests

Holm step-down: sort p-values, multiply the $r$-th smallest by $(m-r+1)$, keep it non-decreasing

The thesis tested BanglaMeta against three baselines on CONF. The raw one-sided p-values (from the 10,000-sample paired bootstrap) are BanglaMeta vs fastText-cc.bn 0.0044, BanglaMeta vs News-W2V 0.0001, BanglaMeta vs Informal-W2V 0.0001. Use $\alpha=0.05$.

(a) Apply the Holm correction. (b) Which hypotheses are confirmed?

Solution

(a) Sort from smallest; multiply by 3, 2, 1; never let an adjusted value fall below the previous one:

  • BanglaMeta vs News-W2V: $(3)\times0.0001=0.0003$ → 0.0003
  • BanglaMeta vs Informal-W2V: $(2)\times0.0001=0.0002$ → 0.0003
  • BanglaMeta vs fastText-cc.bn: $(1)\times0.0044=0.0044$ → 0.0044

(b) All adjusted p-values are below 0.05, so all three are confirmed. These equal the official Holm p-values 0.0044, 0.0003, 0.0003.

Shows: testing three hypotheses at once raises the chance of a false win; Holm controls it, and the thesis result survives the correction.

How much can imputed words move a sentence?

Theorem 8: shift $\le \rho\,\varepsilon$

News-W2V covers 91.2% of informal tokens, so at most $\rho=0.088$ of a typical informal text's tokens need an imputed News row. Suppose each imputed row is within distance $\varepsilon=0.6$ of the row the model would have learned.

(a) Bound how far the sentence vector can move. (b) Why does the bound hold?

Solution

(a) Shift $\le\rho\varepsilon=0.088\times0.6=0.0528$ (in length; a unit row has length 1).

(b) The sentence vector is $\tfrac1T\sum_t r_t$. Only $\rho T$ terms change, each by at most $\varepsilon$; by the triangle inequality the mean changes by at most $\tfrac1T\cdot\rho T\varepsilon=\rho\varepsilon$.

Shows: completing the vocabulary with predicted rows can only move sentence vectors a little, and the classifier's risk by at most $L\Lambda\rho\varepsilon$.

Does a longer vector overfit?

Theorem 9: gap $\le 2L\Lambda/\sqrt n+\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2n)}$, no dimension

Sentence vectors have length at most 1 (a mean of unit rows). A classifier has weight norm $\Lambda=5$, loss Lipschitz constant $L=1$, $n=5{,}950$ texts, $\delta=0.05$.

(a) Compute the bound. (b) How does it change from 300 to 900 or 1,200 dimensions?

Solution

(a) $2\cdot1\cdot5/\sqrt{5950}=0.1296$; $\sqrt{\ln20/11900}=0.0159$; total $0.1455$.

(b) It does not change: the dimension does not appear, only the norms.

Shows: merging to 900 dimensions (or 1,200 for BanglaMeta+) is not by itself a risk of overfitting, as long as rows stay unit-length and the classifier is regularised (cross-validated ridge).

What SVD compression throws away

Theorem 7 (Eckart–Young): residual $=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2$

Two words with 2-dimensional rows form $X=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$.

(a) Find the singular values. (b) Keep only the top direction ($k=1$) and compute the squared error.

Solution

(a) $X$ is symmetric with eigenvectors $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ (eigenvalue 3) and $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$ (eigenvalue 1): $\sigma_1=3,\ \sigma_2=1$.

(b) Projecting each row on $(1,1)/\sqrt2$: $(2,1)\to(1.5,1.5)$ and $(1,2)\to(1.5,1.5)$. Errors $(0.5,-0.5)$ and $(-0.5,0.5)$, squared total $4\times0.25=1.00=\sigma_2^2$.

Shows: SVD keeps the most energy possible, but whatever lies in the dropped directions (here the difference between the two words!) is lost. Since that may be exactly the label information, SVD was tested, not assumed, and it lost (76.36 vs 76.97).
